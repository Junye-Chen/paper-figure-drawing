# Visual explanation, layout and style

These are related decisions made at different resolutions. Establish rough visual character with the core sketch, hierarchy with composition, and a coherent treatment before delivery; do not defer all aesthetic thinking until the last step.

## Visual explanation: how the idea becomes visible

Start from the figure’s job: teaser = intuition first, method = relationships first. Within that role choose by the author-selected relationship and agreed depth. Examples, not fixed presets:

| Scientific relation | Possible visual language | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Multi-input fusion | distinguishable branches converging on a shared output | implying identical inputs |
| Input/output comparison | aligned examples with clear labels | changing conditions between examples |
| Representation transformation | concrete input objects and changed relationships | decorative feature planes with no interpretable change |

For each candidate identify what remains constant and what changes. Use concrete task states to ground abstract entities. No single visual language is mandatory; accept custom sketches and author proposals.

## Composition: where supporting information belongs

Once the core sketch is confirmed, offer appropriate arrangements: concept/evidence, problem/result, comparison, overview/detail, training/inference, parallel branches, feedback, or custom combinations. Preserve accepted user frameworks. Choose hierarchy and area from the approved message, not equal-sized modules by default.

View provided references at readable size. If external visual research is useful, identify what is borrowed (proportion, hierarchy, grouping, typography, color roles) and record the source. Do not borrow another paper's results. Carry a consistent example across panels when it aids understanding; clearly mark illustrative values.

## Style: how the accepted marks look

Show matched-content samples only for unresolved consequential directions. Otherwise implement the established reference/preferences. Overall treatment concerns image/diagram integration, density, emphasis and visual character; routine typography, line widths and spacing are the assistant’s decisions.

Do not label a treatment “beautiful” as evidence of quality. Verify legibility, hierarchy, balance and scientific clarity in the rendered artifact; let the author judge preference.

Meaningful-choice check: can the author see the difference without a narrated list of tiny changes or zooming in? If not, collapse the variants into one recommendation. Typography, border radius, slight color shifts and line widths are implementation details within a direction, not reasons for new options or review loops. Preserve editability so the author can quickly tune these after delivery.

## Establish aesthetic agreement with references

Recommend one primary reference and, only if useful, one supplementary reference. Select for structural relevance first: intuitive task/phenomenon depiction for teasers, clear dependencies and complexity management for methods. Show the actual figure and point to the useful region, stating what to borrow and what not to borrow. Do not send an unfiltered gallery or a font/color questionnaire.

Distinguish expression borrowing (how relations become visible; may shape the core) from aesthetic borrowing (visual weight, density, framing and integration within the accepted layout). A reference can inform both, but author acceptance of one does not authorize replacing the other. Invite overall corrections such as “too abstract” or “images should dominate,” then turn them into a concrete design rather than asking the author to choose implementation details.

Candidate default: one recommendation, plus one genuinely distinct alternative if worthwhile. Teaser alternatives should offer different intuitive entry points; method alternatives should offer different logical organizations. Explain the actual depiction, first reader takeaway and tradeoff. Do not manufacture alternatives just to satisfy a fixed count.
